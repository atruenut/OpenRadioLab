OpenRadio Lab - Getting Started

For complete beginners. No radio or computer-driver knowledge needed.

You can start in 10 seconds with no hardware at all. Run OpenRadioLab.exe and it starts with a built-in simulated radio (clearly labelled "SIMULATED"). Everything on this page about dongles, drivers and DLL files is only needed when you want to listen to real radio.

1. The big picture

A Software Defined Radio (SDR) is a radio where most of the work is done by software instead of by dedicated circuits. A tiny USB "dongle" catches radio waves from an antenna and turns them into a stream of numbers. A program on your computer (this one) turns those numbers into pictures and sound.

 Antenna  ->  USB dongle  ->  USB cable  ->  Windows  ->  OpenRadio Lab
 (catches     (tuner chip +   (carries      (needs to      (draws the
  waves)       converter)      the numbers)   let us in)     spectrum, plays audio)

The most popular dongles are called RTL-SDR. They were originally cheap TV receivers; hobbyists discovered that the chip inside, an RTL2832U, can hand over the raw radio data. That is why they cost about the same as a lunch and why this project starts with them.

2. Why do I need extra files and a driver tool?

This is the question everyone asks, so here is the honest answer. Getting data from the dongle needs three small helpers. Each one solves a specific problem:

Helper Plain-English job Why it is needed
WinUSB driver (installed with a free tool called Zadig) Lets a program talk directly to one specific USB device. Windows decides which "driver" (translator) handles each USB device. For a TV-stick-style dongle it either has none or the wrong kind, and programs are not allowed to talk to the device through it. WinUSB is Microsoft's own generic driver that gives programs direct access.
libusb-1.0.dll The USB plumbing. It handles the low-level conversation between a program and a WinUSB device.
rtlsdr.dll (the librtlsdr library) Knows the dongle's secret language. The RTL2832U chip has its own commands: how to tune, set the sample rate, start streaming. The open-source community figured these out and packaged them in this library. OpenRadio Lab asks the library instead of re-inventing it.

Put together:

OpenRadio Lab  ->  rtlsdr.dll  ->  libusb-1.0.dll  ->  WinUSB driver  ->  your dongle
 (the app)         (speaks RTL)     (speaks USB)        (Windows lets us in)

What is a DLL? A "dynamic link library" is a shared toolbox of code. A program can borrow tools from a DLL that sits in the same folder, which is why the two .dll files must be placed next to OpenRadioLab.exe.

What does Zadig actually change? It tells Windows: "for this one device, use the WinUSB driver". It changes nothing else and you can undo it (see section 7). Always check the device name in the Zadig dropdown before clicking, and never change a keyboard, mouse or USB hub.

Why isn't all of this bundled in the download? They are separate open-source projects with their own licences and release schedules, and the right version matters: RTL-SDR Blog V4 dongles need the RTL-SDR Blog build of the library. Future OpenRadio Lab releases may bundle them; for now you fetch them once.

3. Step by step

Step 1 - Try the simulator first (2 minutes)

  1. Start OpenRadioLab.exe. The banner says SIMULATED SDR.
  2. You will see peaks in the top graph (radio stations) and a scrolling picture below (the waterfall).
  3. Click a big peak: the app measures it. Double-click it to listen (red marker). Turn up Volume.
  4. Press Help any time to reopen this page.

Step 2 - Connect the antenna and dongle

  1. Screw the antenna onto the dongle's connector (usually SMA). Extend a telescopic antenna fully.
  2. Put the antenna near a window, away from computers and chargers (they make radio noise).
  3. Plug the dongle into a USB port. Avoid unpowered hubs. Windows may say it is "setting up a device" and may find nothing: that is expected.

Step 3 - Get the two DLL files

  1. Download the Windows package for the RTL-SDR Blog drivers (see the RTL-SDR Blog Quick Start Guide at rtl-sdr.com). Make sure you are on the real site.
  2. Open the 64-bit (x64) folder. Copy rtlsdr.dll and libusb-1.0.dll.
  3. Paste both into the same folder as OpenRadioLab.exe.

Use the 64-bit files with this 64-bit program. A 32/64-bit mismatch is the most common reason the app says the DLL "was loaded but is missing functions".

Step 4 - Install the WinUSB driver with Zadig

  1. Download Zadig from its official page, zadig.akeo.ie, and run it.
  2. Menu Options > List All Devices.
  3. In the dropdown choose your dongle. It is usually called "Bulk-In, Interface (Interface 0)" (sometimes "RTL2832U"). Pick Interface 0, not Interface 1.
  4. In the driver box on the right make sure the target says WinUSB.
  5. Click Replace Driver (or Install Driver) and wait for "installed successfully".

Step 5 - Start OpenRadio Lab

It detects the dongle automatically and switches from the simulator. The banner now says LIVE RECEIVER and the status bar says Live. If not, press Rescan, then Device report...: it tells you exactly what is missing and what to do.

Step 6 - Your first real listen

  1. Use Quick jump to... > FM broadcast (or type 98.5 and press Enter).
  2. Look for tall peaks about 0.2 MHz apart. Double-click one. The red marker shows where you are listening.
  3. Turn up Volume. If you only hear hiss, double-click a taller peak, or raise the antenna and move it closer to a window.

Keeping the DLLs when you update: OpenRadio Lab also looks for the two DLLs in a libs folder next to the .exe and in %APPDATA%\\OpenRadioLab\\libs (paste that into the Windows Explorer address bar). Put them in the second place once and every future version will find them, so you never have to download them again. (They are not bundled because they are separate projects with their own licences; see THIRD-PARTY.md.)

4. Words you will see

Term Meaning
Spectrum A graph of signal strength at each frequency right now.
Waterfall The spectrum over time, newest at the top; brighter means stronger.
Centre frequency The middle of what the dongle is capturing.
Listening frequency The one you hear (red marker). It can be anywhere inside the capture.
Sample rate (MS/s) Millions of samples per second; also how wide a slice of spectrum you see.
dBFS Signal level where 0 is the strongest the receiver can represent. Weaker is more negative.
Noise floor The ragged baseline of background noise. Signals must rise above it.
WFM / NFM / AM Ways of carrying sound: wide FM (music stations), narrow FM (two-way voice), amplitude (aircraft, AM radio).
Squelch Mutes the audio when the signal is below a level, so you do not hear hiss.

5. Tips for good reception

  • The antenna matters more than the dongle. Higher, near a window, away from electronics.
  • A telescopic antenna works best when its length suits the frequency (the Antenna Workshop will teach this).
  • Too much gain makes strong signals smear across the screen; too little hides weak ones. Beginner mode chooses automatic gain for you.
  • A narrow spike dead in the middle of the screen is not a station. It is a harmless artefact of the receiver.

6. Troubleshooting

What you see Likely cause and fix
"rtlsdr.dll is missing" Copy rtlsdr.dll and libusb-1.0.dll next to the .exe (step 3).
"loaded but missing functions" Wrong DLL or a 32/64-bit mismatch. Use the x64 files.
"wrong driver" / "no working driver" Run Zadig and install WinUSB for the dongle (step 4).
Found but "could not be opened" Close other SDR programs; try another USB port; unplug and replug.
Dongle not found at all Different USB port or cable; avoid hubs; open the Device report.
Works, but only noise Normal indoors. Try the FM preset, a window and a longer antenna.
No sound Check Volume and Mute; in Advanced mode check Audio out; check the squelch is off.
Windows or antivirus warns about a DLL Download only from official project pages and scan the files.
V4 dongle misbehaves Use the RTL-SDR Blog build of the library. HF (below about 24 MHz) is not built in yet.

6b. Only noise on FM (no stations)

First press Scan FM. The app sweeps 88-108 MHz, lists every station it finds in the Stations box, and if it finds none it tells you how far above the noise the best channel was. Then work down this list:

  1. Antenna. Is it screwed in tightly? Extend the telescopic antenna to about 75 cm and put the dongle near a window. No antenna means noise only. This is the most common cause.
  2. Gain. Press Mode: Beginner to switch to Advanced, untick Auto gain, and try 30-45 dB. If wide smeared humps fill the picture, you went too high; back off.
  3. Library mismatch. An RTL-SDR Blog V4 dongle (tuner R828D in the banner) needs a recent RTL-SDR Blog build of rtlsdr.dll; an old generic one can show noise, wrong frequencies or corrupted signals. Get the library from the RTL-SDR Blog's official driver package and replace the old one.
  4. Weak area / indoors. Try a high spot outdoors. Rural FM is often only a few strong stations.
  5. Do not use direct sampling for FM. That mode is for HF and is not what you want here.

7. Undoing the Zadig change

Open Device Manager, find the dongle (under Universal Serial Bus devices), right-click it, choose Uninstall device, tick Delete the driver software if offered, then unplug and replug it.

8. Using radio responsibly

OpenRadio Lab is receive-only: it cannot transmit. Listening to broadcasts, aircraft, weather and amateur radio is a popular hobby, but rules differ by country (in Canada they come from ISED and the Radiocommunication Act). In general: do not share, record or act on private communications, do not try to defeat encryption, and check your local rules. This is general information, not legal advice.
